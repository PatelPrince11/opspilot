I work on the growth team. We run the marketing emails; notification-worker does the sending. This is about the morning in March 2026 when our spring campaign sent people to search and roughly three in ten of them got an error page. Nothing was broken. A limit did exactly what it was built to do, at a size nobody had looked at in a long time.

First, how search traffic gets in. Every request passes through edge-gateway, three Envoy pods that appear in the logs as edge-gateway-01, -02 and -03. On the `/search` route, and only that route, each pod runs a local rate limiter: a bucket of tokens that refills by a fixed amount every second. A request that finds the bucket empty is turned away on the spot with status `429`, before search-api ever hears about it. Our edge marks those rejections in the response body, which carries the code `EDGE-RL01`.

Two things about that limiter caught us out. The number is per pod. In March it was 100 tokens a second on each of the three, so the whole edge let through about 300 searches a second, and only if traffic spread evenly. And the limit went in when the route was first set up, as a guard against crawlers, sized for the traffic of that time. Search traffic had grown since. The limit hadn't.

On 2026-03-10 the campaign started sending at 09:00. The first `429`s showed up at 09:05, and by 09:10 `/search` had peaked at about 420 requests a second. With 300 allowed, about 120 a second were refused, around 29%, or roughly 40 a second on each pod. team-catalog was paged at 09:12 and pulled us in once it was clear the traffic came from our send.

The first twenty minutes were confusing because every search system looked healthy. search-api had no errors, and its request rate sat flat at about 300 a second: the 420 arriving, minus the 120 the edge refused. search-cluster had plenty of headroom, with no rejections in its search thread pools, and nobody saw `SRCH-E502`. As far as search-api could tell nothing was wrong, because the refused requests never reached it. The problem was visible in one place only, the edge.

Once someone filtered the edge access logs to `/search`, it was obvious: a wall of `429`s, every body carrying `EDGE-RL01`. The limiter keeps its own counters too, which you can read on each edge pod through Envoy's admin port:

```
kubectl -n platform port-forward pod/<edge pod> 9901:9901
curl -s localhost:9901/stats | grep http_local_rate_limit
```

The `enforced` counter (requests that actually got the `429`) was rising on all three pods at about the same pace. If it had risen on one pod only, traffic would have been landing unevenly, and a bigger limit wouldn't have been the right fix.

team-catalog checked that search-cluster could absorb the extra load (it could, at around 40% CPU) and raised the limit to 250 a second per pod. That's 750 for the edge, about 1.8 times the campaign's peak. The limiter's settings live in the ConfigMap `edge-gateway-envoy`, and Envoy reads them only at startup, so the change meant a rolling restart of edge-gateway. It merged at 09:42, rolled out from 09:46, and the `429`s stopped at 09:50: forty-five minutes in all.

What I'd like other teams to take from this, especially people who aren't SREs:

A `429` can mean someone's limit, not a broken service. If users get `429` on search while search-api is calm, look at the edge first.

Per-pod limits add up. Change the number of edge pods, or how evenly traffic spreads across them, and the real limit changes with it.

Every limit needs an owner and a date to look at it again. This one had neither, so nobody remembered it existed until our users hit it.

And for us: big sends now come with a heads-up. Before a campaign we expect to bring a crowd, we tell team-catalog the send time and our best guess at the traffic, so they can check the limit and the cluster before we press send.

None of the other edge routes, checkout and payments included, has a local limit like this one.

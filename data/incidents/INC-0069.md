# Postmortem: a night of OOMKilled restarts on orders-api-03

sev2 · 2025-08-09 23:25 to 2025-08-10 11:35 UTC · owner: team-commerce · runbooks: RB-004, RB-005

## The shape of the incident

From 23:25 on the evening of 9 August 2025 until 11:35 the next morning, a stretch of twelve hours and ten minutes, one orders-api pod kept dying. orders-api-03 was terminated by the kernel's memory killer more than once, and each time Kubernetes reported the container's last state as `OOMKilled`. Memory climbed again after the restarts. No restart count was kept, so none is given here.

## Reading the symptom

A container killed for passing its memory limit exits with 137 and the reason `OOMKilled`. A kill after a missed shutdown deadline also exits with 137 but carries another reason, so the reason is the field that tells the cases apart (RB-005). Here the reason was `OOMKilled`.

The incident is a growing JavaScript heap on one pod. RB-004 is the orders-api checklist for that; its standard sets the runtime heap flag, `--max-old-space-size`, to about 75% of the container limit. Why the kills still arrived as `OOMKilled` is not known.

## Evidence that was available

orders-api starts with `--heapsnapshot-signal=SIGUSR2`, so a snapshot can be written from a running pod, and the files land in the working directory `/app`. RB-004 recommends taking one well before the limit, because writing a snapshot costs extra memory. No snapshot from this night exists, which leaves the growing objects and the code that allocated them unidentified.

## Scope and timing

Only orders-api-03 is named for this incident. The service runs on several pods, and what the others did over the night is not known. The only two clock times on record are the start at 23:25 and the end at 11:35, so the individual kills carry no times here. Because the window crosses midnight, the kills fall on two calendar dates, 9 and 10 August.

## Impact

Each kill is abrupt: the container gets no warning, and whatever the pod was serving at that moment goes with it. No failure count or customer figure was kept, and the incident is rated sev2.

## Response

The on-call was on duty for the incident from the start, and the kills had stopped by 11:35. Nothing is written down about a mitigation or about whether a release was suspected.

## Lessons

- The incident ran for twelve hours on one pod without a snapshot being taken, even though the signal flag was already set at startup.
- With no heap evidence, the cause stays at "the heap grew".
- A snapshot early in a climb would have turned this into a finding.
- The pod's own restart counter holds the number of restarts and should be copied into the ticket.
